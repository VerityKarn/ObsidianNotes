# Overview
Information and war have always had a close relationship. Sun Tzu's famous/infamous *The Art of War* stressed the need for dedicated troops to intelligence gathering, and the use of deception in battle. Diversionary tactics have been in use since militaries and militias have existed. Equally, propaganda has been found to exist throughout the entirety of the human record.

Yet, in the context of our current information age, these elements have now seeped into the very fabric of everyday life. Nearly every aspect of one's own personal life has had some breach of the public discourse, and the problem is only getting worse. [Even South Park had to explain that "politics became pop culture," when figuring out how to approach their latest season](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/11/08/business/media/south-park-trump.html). Yet, when nearly everything becomes enveloped by the political sphere, then the surface area of disinformation attacks is no longer contained within the sphere of either military or intelligence. Rather, the surface area of a disinformation attack nearly encompasses the entirety of daily activity. From gasoline to groceries, there is always some element of potential disinformation to be spread.

Examining how Russia utilizes disinformation, the pattern clearly shows how they've expanded their areas of focus. By co-opting anti-vaxers, holocaust deniers, race advocacy groups, and flat earthers, Russia has made attempts to undermine trust in almost every institution. Yet, this is not unique to Russia's history, but rather the modern expansion of previous Russian engagements on the world stage.

Here, we provide a history of Russian disinformation, and how it eventually evolved into the current world we live in. We contrast this, in part, with disinfo campaigns ran by China and Iran, to demonstrate how Russian disinfo is uniquely dangerous to discourse within the US and Western Europe. We then make an argument for the position of the broader academic community, and how they are uniquely positioned. We focus on how they are the first recipients of distrust, but also situated in the strongest position to retaliate against these disinfo campaigns.

We also, regrettably, argue for a rather pessimistic outlook on the overall situation. Information warfare, by design, creates an environment in which even well educated and intelligent individuals can be ensnared. The capitulation or manufacturing of expertise creates an environment in which poor or compromised information could potentially circulate indefinitely throughout one's information sphere/infosphere.

As such, it is the author's prerogative to explain that academia's role is to not view itself as a disconnected arm from political or even military realities. Academia, and the institutions that make up what we broadly confer as such, are considered to be genuine military targets of disinformation campaigns. Even seemingly disconnected fields, such as biology and chemistry, are continually brought into question through disinfo campaigns. Academia can not view itself as disconnected from the reality of an enemy that wishes to undermine it. It must see itself as both victim of these attacks, but simultaneously the most capable of repelling such attacks. Academia must understand that, whether it likes it or not, it is engaged in war.

## Defining the terms
While some of these terms may appear intuitive, much of the controversy that surrounds them is due to how they relate and/or interplay with one another. For example, is Information Warfare a subset of Cyber warfare? Is it the reverse? Or do these have distinct domains that tend to overlap? Equally, there are further divisions to these terms that require further parsing. If a network is penetrated, but only data is stolen, then would we call it a "cyberattack," or is it just "cyberespionage?" Some argue that it's both, while others argue that a clear distinction needs to be made. Much of this section is made to primarily get the reader to understand how I'm tackling these terms, but also explaining that they are not concretely agreed upon terms.
